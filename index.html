Three ideas first

A black hole is a lot of stuff in a tiny space.

Its gravity is so strong that not even light gets out. Pick something below to squeeze.

About a dime
All of Earth, squeezed to 0.7 inches across, almost exactly a dime.

Heavy things bend space.

Like a weight on a trampoline. Swing heavy things around fast and the dents ripple outward. Those ripples are gravitational waves.

A deep dip, enough to keep every planet circling the Sun.

We can finally feel the ripples.

LIGO caught its first in 2015, from two black holes that collided over a billion years ago. It won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.

LIGO has two giant L-shaped detectors, one in Washington and one in Louisiana. A passing ripple stretches one arm of the L and squeezes the other, and lasers measure the change. This is what both recorded on September 14, 2015.

Up and down is the stretch. At its biggest, the 2.5-mile arms changed length by less than a hundredth of a proton’s width. Two detectors far apart saw the same thing, so it was real. The sound is the real recording, raised in pitch.

1Spiral in

The hum of giant black holes

In act one, two black holes circle each other, getting closer and faster.

My part: working out how many giant black hole pairs are out there, and how big they are, from the faint ripples they all make together.

Most big galaxies have a giant black hole at the center, millions to billions of times heavier than the Sun. When galaxies merge, the two giants pair up and circle each other for millions of years.

One of their ripples takes years to pass, too slow for any detector on Earth. So astronomers time pulsars, dead stars that flash like lighthouses with near-perfect regularity. A passing ripple makes the flashes arrive early or late.

The wave jiggles the pulsar toward and away from us, so its flashes land early or late. Each dot is one flash. The real wobble is far too small to see, so it’s blown up here.

In 2023 the NANOGrav team, which times dozens of pulsars, found signs of a faint, steady “hum” of these ripples, most likely from all the giant pairs at once. It isn’t a sound. It’s a background of countless overlapping waves, like the murmur of a crowd.

I build pretend universes full of giant pairs and check which ones make the hum we measure.

One pair makes a clean wave, easy to pick out. Now add more.

Why it matters

The hum is a record of galaxies colliding and growing over billions of years, written in ripples instead of light.

2Crash

Crashing black holes on a supercomputer

In act two, the black holes crash and become one, in a fraction of a second.

My part: crashing black holes together inside a supercomputer. My twist is that my black holes carry electric charge, the same stuff behind static electricity. Real ones probably carry very little, but nobody knows for sure.

Einstein’s equations for a collision are too tangled to solve with pencil and paper. Instead, a supercomputer chops space into boxes, like the pixels of a photo, and keeps track of how bent space is in each one. Then it steps everything forward in time, a sliver at a time. The boxes get smaller near the black holes, where things change fastest.

The black circles are the black holes, at their true size. The smallest boxes crowd around them, where things change fastest. Zoomed in 130 times.

3Ring down

A shortcut for the ringing

In act three, the new black hole rings like a struck bell, then goes quiet.

My part: a shortcut formula that predicts the ringing in a split second, instead of days on a supercomputer.

Detectors need a huge library of possible signals to compare against, and each supercomputer run takes days. The shortcut is a formula nicknamed BOB.

My job is making the formula match supercomputer crashes like the ones in act two. Try it yourself: find the right pitch and fade.

In real life, the pitch and fade aren’t guesses. They’re set by the new black hole’s mass, spin and charge, and the work is getting the formula right, ringing and all, for every kind of collision.

Why it matters

Every new detection is identified by comparing it with predicted signals. Faster, more accurate formulas mean more collisions found, and sharper measurements of the black holes in them.

Think of it like

A song-recognition app. It can name a song because it already knows what every song sounds like. Detectors need the same kind of songbook for black hole collisions, and a fast, accurate formula is how you fill it.

Words you might hear me use

Gravitational waves
Ripples in space itself, made by heavy things moving fast.
LIGO
Two giant L-shaped detectors, in Washington and Louisiana, that feel gravitational waves passing Earth.
Merger
The moment two black holes become one.
Ringdown
The fading ring of the new black hole right after a merger.
Waveform
The ripple signal drawn as a wiggly line over time, like the one under the animation at the top.
Pulsar
A burned-out star that spins and flashes like a lighthouse, keeping near-perfect time.
Spin
Black holes spin, some of them close to the fastest rate nature allows.
A run
One supercomputer simulation. “My run crashed” almost always means the computer program, not the black holes.
Mismatch
A score for how far my formula is from the supercomputer’s answer, like in the game above. 0 is a perfect match.
